APEX AI: Human vs. AI Racing

Training a reinforcement learning agent to beat human lap times in CarRacing-v3

Aldorino Gladwyn, Mikhail Lazarev, Maksim Lovchev, Hong Nam Hoang
Group 2 — University of Technology Sydney — 41118 Artificial Intelligent in Robotics

Abstract

APEX AI is a reinforcement learning project built on OpenAI Gymnasium's CarRacing-v3 environment. Our goal is to train an AI agent that can drive a simulated race car and outperform a human driver — measured in lap time across two different track configurations.

The agent perceives the environment through a 96×96 pixel grayscale camera feed with no access to GPS, a map, or any privileged information. A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) extracts spatial features from each frame, which are then passed to a Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) algorithm that outputs continuous control signals for steering, acceleration, and braking.

We establish a human benchmark using a custom manual driving interface with WASD controls and a live leaderboard system. The trained AI is then evaluated against this benchmark on the same tracks. After 800,000 training steps, the agent achieved competitive lap times — winning on Map 2 and coming within 0.17 seconds of the human best on Map 1.

How It Works

The system follows a standard deep reinforcement learning pipeline:

  1. Observation: At each timestep, the CarRacing-v3 environment provides a 96×96 RGB frame. We convert this to grayscale to reduce input complexity while preserving track geometry information.
  2. Feature Extraction: A CNN processes the grayscale frame and extracts spatial features — such as track edges, upcoming corners, and car position — that the raw pixel values alone cannot convey directly.
  3. Decision Making: The extracted features are passed to the PPO agent, which outputs a continuous action: how much to steer (left/right), how much to accelerate, and how hard to brake.
  4. Reward Signal: The environment returns a shaped reward at every timestep based on track progress, speed, and driving behaviour. The agent updates its policy to maximise cumulative reward over time.
  5. Human Benchmark: A separate manual driving mode lets team members drive the car using WASD controls. Lap times are saved to a leaderboard, establishing the human target the AI must beat.
Training setup: PPO with CNN feature extractor via Stable-Baselines3, trained on CPU for 800,000 timesteps. Checkpoints saved every 50,000 steps. A minimum gas floor and action assist wrappers were applied to prevent stalling and spinning in place during early training.

Reward Shaping

Rather than relying on the sparse default reward, we implemented a custom reward shaping system to guide the agent toward better driving behaviour:

Rewards

  • Progress bonus — reward for each new track tile visited
  • Speed reward — encourages the car to move fast
  • Completion bonus — bonus for finishing the full lap

Penalties

  • Steering smoothness — penalises jerky/erratic steering
  • Off-track penalty — penalises when all 4 wheels leave the track
  • No-progress penalty — penalises when the car stops advancing
  • Time penalty — small penalty each timestep to encourage speed
  • Termination penalty — large penalty for spinning in place too long

Results

Training Progress

Training was conducted over 800,000 timesteps on CPU. Early in training the agent exhibited erratic behaviour — slow, shaky steering and frequent track departures. As training progressed the agent stabilised significantly, completing full laps consistently and achieving competitive lap times on both maps.

Discussion

Training Duration and Stability

At 800,000 training steps, the agent demonstrated competitive but not yet dominant performance. Early training showed erratic, shaky behaviour typical of an unexplored policy. As training progressed, the reward shaping system — particularly the no-progress termination penalty and steering smoothness penalty — guided the agent toward more stable and deliberate driving. Further training beyond 800k steps would likely improve consistency and close the remaining gap on Map 1.

Effect of Reward Shaping

The custom reward shaping system played a critical role in the agent's development. Without it, the agent would often discover unintended shortcuts — such as spinning in circles to accumulate small tile rewards without actually completing a lap. The combination of a no-progress termination penalty and an off-track penalty (triggered when all 4 wheels leave the road) effectively eliminated these degenerate behaviours. Meanwhile, the speed reward and completion bonus encouraged the agent to drive fast and finish laps rather than stalling.

Corner Cutting as a Racing Strategy

One notable behaviour observed in the trained agent is a tendency to cut corners — taking a tighter inside line through turns rather than following the road precisely. While this may initially appear as a flaw, it is in fact a valid and well-established racing strategy. In Formula 1, drivers routinely cut the apex of a corner to maintain higher speed through the turn. The agent appears to have independently discovered this strategy through reinforcement learning, without any explicit instruction.

Braking Behaviour

Despite competitive lap times, braking remains the least refined aspect of the agent's driving. The agent tends to brake late or inconsistently on tighter corners. This is likely a consequence of the speed reward — the agent has learned that maintaining speed generally produces better rewards, even if it sometimes leads to imprecise cornering. Future work could address this through additional reward shaping targeting corner entry speed.

Map 1 — A Very Close Race

On Map 1, the AI's best time of 17.75s beat the human best of 17.92s by just 0.17 seconds — an extremely tight margin. This shows the agent is operating at near-human level on this track. With additional training steps, the AI would likely pull further ahead as the policy continues to refine corner entry and braking points.

Limitations and Future Work

  • CPU-only training: All training was conducted on CPU, limiting the number of steps and hyperparameter configurations we could explore.
  • 800k steps: Further training would likely improve performance, particularly on Map 1 where the margin is very small.
  • Braking refinement: Targeted reward shaping for corner entry speed could produce smoother, faster cornering.
  • Generalisation: The agent was evaluated on the same maps used during training. Testing on unseen tracks would be a stronger measure of generalisation.
