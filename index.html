APEX AI: Human vs. AI Racing

Training a reinforcement learning agent to beat human lap times in CarRacing-v3

Jonathan, Aldorino, Maksim, Mikhail, Hong
Group 2 — University of Technology Sydney — 41118 Intelligent Robotics
Code

After 2 million training steps, our PPO agent beats the human benchmark on all three maps — in both lap time and coin collection.

Abstract

APEX AI is a reinforcement learning project built on OpenAI Gymnasium's CarRacing-v3 environment. Our goal is to train an AI agent that can drive a simulated race car and outperform a human driver — measured in both lap time and coin collection across three different track configurations.

The agent perceives the environment through a 96×96 pixel grayscale camera feed with no access to GPS, a map, or any privileged information. A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) extracts spatial features from each frame, which are then passed to a Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) algorithm that outputs continuous control signals for steering, acceleration, and braking.

We establish a human benchmark using a custom manual driving interface with WASD controls and a live leaderboard system. The trained AI is then evaluated against this benchmark on the same tracks. After 2 million training steps, the agent exceeded our expectations — surpassing the human benchmark on all maps in both evaluation modes.

How It Works

The system follows a standard deep reinforcement learning pipeline:

  1. Observation: At each timestep, the CarRacing-v3 environment provides a 96×96 RGB frame. We convert this to grayscale to reduce input complexity while preserving track geometry information.
  2. Feature Extraction: A CNN processes the grayscale frame and extracts spatial features — such as track edges, upcoming corners, and car position — that the raw pixel values alone cannot convey directly.
  3. Decision Making: The extracted features are passed to the PPO agent, which outputs a continuous action: how much to steer (left/right), how much to accelerate, and how hard to brake.
  4. Environment Feedback: The environment returns a reward signal based on track tiles visited and speed. The agent updates its policy to maximise cumulative reward over time.
  5. Human Benchmark: A separate manual driving mode lets team members drive the car using WASD controls. Lap times and coin scores are saved to a leaderboard, establishing the human target the AI must beat.
Training setup: PPO with CNN feature extractor via Stable-Baselines3, trained on CPU for 2,000,000 timesteps. Checkpoints saved every 50,000 steps. A minimum gas floor of 0.3 was applied to prevent the agent from stalling and spinning in place during early training.

Results

Training Progress

Training was conducted in two key phases. At 250,000 steps, the agent showed early but unstable behaviour — the car moved forward but struggled to stay on track, frequently overcorrecting on corners and moving slowly. By 2,000,000 steps, the agent had stabilised significantly, completing full laps consistently and achieving competitive lap times across all three maps.

Discussion

Training Duration and Stability

The most significant factor in agent performance was training duration. At 250,000 steps, the agent's behaviour was visibly erratic — slow, shaky steering, and frequent track departures. This is consistent with early-stage PPO training where the policy has not yet converged. By 2,000,000 steps, the agent's driving became noticeably smoother and more deliberate, demonstrating that sufficient exploration and policy updates are critical in continuous control tasks.

Corner Cutting

One interesting behaviour observed in the trained agent is a tendency to cut corners — taking a tighter line through turns rather than following the road precisely. While this may appear as a flaw, it is in fact a valid and commonly used racing strategy. In Formula 1 and other motorsports, drivers regularly cut the apex of a corner to maintain higher speed through the turn. As long as the car does not leave the track entirely, corner cutting is an accepted performance optimisation. Our evaluation criteria reflects this — the AI is assessed on lap time and coin collection, not on strict adherence to lane position.

Braking Behaviour

Despite strong overall performance, braking remains the most underdeveloped aspect of the agent's driving. The agent tends to brake late or inconsistently, particularly on tighter corners. This is likely a result of the reward structure — the environment rewards visiting track tiles quickly, which incentivises speed over careful deceleration. Future work could address this through reward shaping — adding a penalty for excessive speed entering tight corners — or by extending training further to allow the agent more time to discover the value of controlled braking.

Exceeding Expectations

The agent's performance across all three maps exceeded our initial expectations, particularly on lap time. We anticipated the AI would require significantly more tuning to beat human performance, but the combination of CNN feature extraction and PPO's stable policy updates proved effective even on CPU hardware. This demonstrates the practical applicability of off-the-shelf deep RL algorithms for continuous control tasks.

Limitations and Future Work

Several limitations remain. Training was conducted on CPU, making iteration slow and limiting the number of hyperparameter configurations we could explore. The agent was also trained without a reward shaping component, meaning all learning came from the sparse tile-based reward signal. Future improvements could include:

  • GPU-accelerated training for faster iteration
  • Reward shaping to encourage smoother braking and more consistent cornering
  • Curriculum learning — starting on simpler track segments before progressing to full laps
  • Hyperparameter tuning for learning rate, entropy coefficient, and network architecture
